Tsetlin Machine research · the count floor

Every next word,
a sum of votes you can read.

Before a language model made of logical clauses can matter, it has to beat a model that only counts. This page builds that model in your tab: it reads ten public-domain books, counts every pattern of up to five words, and predicts the next word as an exact sum of clause votes, each one traced back to the pages it was counted from.

1 · the glass box

Write, and read why it guesses what it guesses

Each guess is a vote. A pattern it counted in the books, like “the last two words were best of”, is a clause: it fires when your text ends that way and votes for the words that came next. Your own text is a second source: a memory of what you have written so far.

    3 · the floor, measured in this tab

    How surprised is it by chapters it never counted?

    Nineteen chapters were held out and never counted. The page scores every word of them, once with each amount of context and once with the memory of the chapter so far. Lower is better: perplexity is the number of words it is, on average, choosing between.

    The second study, hierarchical_tm, ended mostly negative as well: its single final test scored AUC 0.735 on same-article pairs, below 0.817 for plain word overlap. A reproduction check after the study re-checked 124 of the 683 numbers of its results page, all exactly.

    The third study, clause_lm, prepares a word-level model whose clauses are AND-rules over OR-groups of words. Its one registered question, about the parameter s of a Tsetlin router on CLINC150 (M5 step 1), has a negative answer by its own rules (mean test accuracy 0.8201 against the required 0.848), resting on a gate that was amended after its first result; the language-model steps M1 to M4 have not run. Of its 12 ids, 4 are met, 3 not met and 5 not run.

    The fourth study, decision_layer, asks where Tsetlin Machines fail on language and what works when an encoder does the language and a Tsetlin Machine makes the decision: 72 of its 107 registered ids are met, 32 not met and 3 not run. Agents that choose the next action in customer-service dialogues come within the registered 2-point margin of an agent built on the bge-large encoder without a neural network at run time (STAR +0.06, ABCD -1.00 points, R19) and, with a word table counted from C4 text, without one at any stage (within 0.10 and 0.29 points of the Model2Vec agent, R21). The Tsetlin Machine itself often stays below logistic regression on the same bits (1.55 to 2.49 points on MultiWOZ, R24), and the STAR and ABCD test splits had been read many times before R22 and R23.

    The fifth study, tally, repeated H8.1 with the Tsetlin Machine library of the CAIR group (tmu) on Penn Treebank: here the learned votes beat the counted read-out of the same clauses by 0.25 bits per word (+0.251, 95% interval +0.222 to +0.280, registered test T0, not met), the opposite of H8.1, and logistic regression on the same clause outputs is far ahead of both. Its count floor, which has no Tsetlin Machine in it, gained 7.59% from an exchange-class code added to a Kneser-Ney 5-gram with a cache (A2, met on its dev-test split).

    Every id, qualifier and run is in the results overview.

    Read this before quoting a number

    • A reimplementation, checked. The model on this page is the CVTM-core of the study (section 11 of its engine specification) written again in JavaScript. A test compares it with the study's own Python code on the same tokens: at 2,500 sampled held-out positions every probability agrees to within 4 × 10−15, and over all 58,266 positions the totals agree to within 10−9.
    • A different corpus. Ten public-domain books, lowercase words, 678,041 counted tokens. The study used WikiText-103 with byte-level subwords. Perplexities here are per word and are not comparable with its bits per byte.
    • No Tsetlin Machine runs here. This is the floor the study measured its Tsetlin Machine language model against. That model stayed above it, which here means worse. The count floor that the programme designs for its later studies adds word-category codes, which this page does not have.
    • Fitted the study's way. The weights of the memory come from the study's MAP-EM on 14 training chapters, chosen by the study's residue rule (10 percent instead of 1, because there are 208 chapters instead of 28,472 articles).
    • New words. A word the books never use gets one of 256 slots: the counts have never seen it, the memory can learn it from your text. A held-out chapter has at most 160 such words; in a text with more than 256, the later ones share the last slot.
